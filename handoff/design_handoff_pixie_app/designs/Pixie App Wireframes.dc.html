73Client · the change flow, and the collision

Two of turn 72's screens make "Change booking" their primary action and it currently leads nowhere — the old reschedule flow was drawn under the refundable model. Building the destination surfaced the answer to conflict ④, so it's proposed here rather than left as a flag: a time change and a route change are not the same transaction. Moving your pickup on the same route costs nothing, so it can stay free right up to the 2-hour boundary. Changing where you're going re-prices the trip, and a price difference has to settle — which it can't after 6pm the day before. So the 2-hour deadline governs time and date; the payment cutoff governs anything that moves money. One flow, two ceilings, stated up front so nobody discovers it at the end.

68Dispatch · the request nobody claimed

The failure the access model creates, and the only one with no customer screen at all — a customer who can't reach her trip can't be shown anything. Dana requested on a mistyped number, dispatch confirmed inside 15 minutes, payment came due, and nobody signed in. Today that follows the same path as 27a: the hold lapses, the trip quietly dies, and she walks out of MCO at midnight to nobody. So the actor here is dispatch, and this turn is a new surface — an ops console, drawn in a phone for canvas consistency but really a desktop tool. Money appears on these screens; that's correct, dispatch takes payment. The driver-side no-money rule is untouched.

Two rules govern this turn. Payment must clear by 6pm the day before pickup — that's when dispatch locks the next night's roster, since shifts run 6pm–3am and tomorrow's first pickup is already assigned by the end of one (57a). Nothing is ever collected at the kerb or in the car, so past that hour an unpaid trip simply cannot run. And the 20-minute driver hold must not start at confirmation; it must start when the trip is claimed. Same-day requests are the one exception — accepted only when a driver is already free, with payment due on confirmation and no grace. Someone who can't see the pay screen is not the same as someone who won't act on it, and 27a currently punishes both identically. Confirm → wait for claim → then hold and charge.

68aThe unclaimed queue

Sorted by how close the cutoff is, not how old the request is — payment is due by 6pm the day before pickup, so a trip tonight is already past saving by ordinary means and one in three weeks is admin. That single sort decision is most of the value on this screen. Each row carries the only three things dispatch can act on: the name, the number given, and the flight. There is no orange on this screen at all — nothing here is the forward action by itself, and urgency is carried by weight and elevation rather than colour. The soonest pickup gets display type, a lifted shadow and a filled navy chip; the rest sit quiet.

67Client · the number is wrong

The last hole in the access model, and the sharpest. Because the mobile number is the account and nothing is ever sent by text or link, one mistyped digit doesn't degrade the experience — it severs it completely. The request exists, dispatch confirms it, and the customer has no route to it: no email, no link, no SMS. She finds out at baggage claim. So the design puts almost all its weight on prevention (67a) and treats the human phone call as the only real recovery, reached fast and without shame (67b). It also closes a quieter hole: a typo'd number belongs to somebody, so a stranger must never be handed a trip just for signing in (67c).

The alternative worth weighing: send the code at request time and require it to submit. That eliminates this entire turn — but it gates the one step that's currently open to anyone, and a family comparing prices at 11pm would have to verify a phone number before seeing a car. My read: keep requesting open, spend a screen on read-back instead. Worth ratifying either way.

66Client · a change that costs less

The mirror of 17b, and the last gap in the flow. Because the ride is paid in full at confirm, a cheaper change means Pixie owes money back mid-booking — the only place in the product where that happens. The temptation is to make it feel like a win: confetti, "you saved $35!", a nudge to spend it. That's the wrong instinct. A flat price that goes down when the trip gets shorter isn't a discount, it's the flat price working correctly, and treating it as a gift implies the original number was padded.
